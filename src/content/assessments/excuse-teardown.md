## Instructions

> Take one real excuse apart and show your working.

Pick a real excuse, whether your own, a public figure's or a company's, and analyse it
using the vocabulary built in weeks 1–3: the taxonomy of denial,
justification, and diffusion, and the grammatical devices (passive voice,
hedges, nominalisation) from week 2. The strongest submissions show where the
excuse resists the taxonomy, not just where it fits it.

Choose an excuse with enough length and stakes to actually contain multiple
moves. A single-sentence text message apology will not give you enough
material to demonstrate depth of analysis, however cleanly it fits a
category. A public statement, a formal letter, or a recorded and
transcribed remark gives you sentence-level detail to work with and a
verifiable source to cite. As you work through it, resist settling for the
first taxonomy label that fits: this is a teardown, not a tagging exercise,
so treat an easy classification as the start of the analysis rather than
its conclusion. Ask what the excuse is doing that the taxonomy doesn't
neatly capture, such as a justification that only partially succeeds, or a
denial that undercuts itself in a later sentence, and say so explicitly rather
than smoothing the analysis over.

Ground every claim about a grammatical device in the excuse's actual
wording. "The excuse uses hedging language" is an assertion; "the phrase
'may have contributed' hedges causal responsibility by declining to
commit to whether a contribution occurred at all" is analysis. The
difference between the two is the entire point of this assignment, and it
is the single clearest signal markers use to separate a Distinction
analysis from a Credit one.

## Submission Requirements

A 600–800 word written analysis, quoting the excuse in full at the top, with
its source cited. Reference the taxonomy and the grammar devices by name.
The word count is a ceiling as much as a target: at this length there is no
room to re-summarise the excuse before analysing it, so move from the
quoted text directly into argument. Cite the source precisely enough that
a reader could locate the original (a URL, a publication and date, a named
recording). An excuse that can't be verified against its stated source
will be treated as unverifiable regardless of how good the analysis around
it is.

## Marking rubric

Each criterion is judged against the scale below, not allocated a mark
count. Your grade is the band your work sits in, not an average of parts.

| Criteria | High Distinction (5) | Distinction (4) | Credit (3) | Pass (2) | Fail (1) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Accuracy of the taxonomy applied** | Every instance of denial, justification, and diffusion in the excuse is identified correctly, including places where the mechanisms mix or work against each other. | The taxonomy is applied correctly to the excuse's main moves, with at most one minor misclassification. | The dominant taxonomy category is identified correctly, but secondary or mixed mechanisms are missed or misnamed. | The taxonomy is invoked but misapplied to at least one major move in the excuse, or asserted without being shown in the text. | The excuse is not analysed against the course's taxonomy, or the taxonomy is used incorrectly throughout. |
| **Depth of the grammatical analysis** | At least two sentence-level devices from week 2 (passive voice, hedges, nominalisation) are identified with the exact wording quoted, and each is tied to the specific blame it moves or absorbs. | Two devices are identified and correctly quoted, with a clear account of what each does to the excuse's meaning. | At least one device is correctly identified and quoted, with a plausible account of its effect. | A device is named but not clearly located in the excuse's actual wording, or its effect is asserted rather than explained. | No sentence-level device from week 2 is identified, or the analysis stays at the level of general impression rather than specific wording. |
| **Clarity of writing** | The analysis is tightly argued within the word count, moves from claim to quoted evidence without padding, and reads as though every sentence earns its place. | The analysis is clearly organised and readable, with only occasional redundancy or imprecise phrasing. | The analysis is readable but uneven: some paragraphs argue clearly, others restate the excuse rather than analysing it. | The writing is difficult to follow, or spends more of the word count summarising the excuse than analysing it. | The writing is unclear enough that the analysis's claims cannot be reliably followed. |
