## Instructions

> Find a company's non-apology, and write the apology it should have been.

Corporate statements are the purest form of the non-apology, because they are
drafted by people who know exactly what they're avoiding. Find one, quote it
in full, and rewrite it as a statement that would actually count as an
apology under week 5's definition — then explain every change you made and
why.

Look for a statement issued in response to a specific, identifiable
failure — a data breach notification, a product recall, a service outage
apology — rather than a generic goodwill press release, since the lab
depends on there being a real harm the original statement is visibly
avoiding naming. Before rewriting, diagnose the original in full: locate
every passive construction that removes an actor, every hedge that
softens a certainty into a possibility, every agentless nominalisation
("mistakes were made", "an issue occurred") that turns an action into an
event with no one behind it. The rewrite is only as good as this
diagnosis — a rewrite produced by intuition, without first cataloguing what
the original was doing, tends to fix the obvious passive voice while
leaving subtler hedges intact.

When rewriting, hold yourself to week 5's actual definition of a genuine
apology, not a looser folk sense of "sounds sincere." That means naming
the specific harm rather than a vague reference to "the situation," using
active constructions that name who is responsible, and committing to a
remedy specific enough that its completion could later be checked. A
rewrite that sounds warmer than the original but still declines to name
who did what, or still leaves the remedy open-ended, has not actually
crossed the threshold the lab is testing for.

## Submission Requirements

The original (quoted, cited), your rewrite, and a commentary of 300–400 words
naming each device you removed (passive voice, hedges, agentless
constructions) and what you replaced it with. Present the original and the
rewrite in full so a marker can compare them sentence by sentence, then use
the commentary to do the analytical work explicitly — a table or paired
list mapping each removed device to its replacement is acceptable and
often clearer than prose at this length. The commentary should explain not
just what changed but why the replacement satisfies week 5's standard,
since a rewrite that merely sounds different without a stated rationale
does not demonstrate the analysis the lab is assessing.

## Marking rubric

Each criterion is judged against the scale below, not allocated a mark
count — your grade is the band your work sits in, not an average of parts.

| Criteria | High Distinction (5) | Distinction (4) | Credit (3) | Pass (2) | Fail (1) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Accuracy of the original teardown** | Every non-apology device in the original (passive voice, hedges, agentless constructions, misplaced conditionals) is identified with the exact wording quoted. | Most devices in the original are correctly identified and quoted, with at most one missed. | The original's most obvious device is identified correctly, but subtler ones are missed. | A device is named but not clearly located in the original's actual wording. | The original is not analysed for its non-apology devices, or the analysis misidentifies what the devices are. |
| **Quality of the rewrite as a genuine apology** | The rewrite would pass week 5's test for a genuine apology without qualification — it names the harm, takes responsibility without hedging, and commits to a specific remedy. | The rewrite meets week 5's standard on all but a minor point, such as a slightly vague remedy. | The rewrite improves clearly on the original but still hedges responsibility or leaves the remedy unspecified. | The rewrite removes some devices but reintroduces others, or the apology remains vague about who is responsible. | The rewrite does not meaningfully differ from the original in substance, only in wording. |
| **Clarity of the commentary explaining the change** | Every device removed is named and matched to what replaced it, with a clear account of why the replacement counts as more honest. | Most changes are named and explained clearly, with at most one left unaccounted for. | The commentary names the main changes but the explanation of why they matter is thin or generic. | The commentary describes the rewrite generally without tying specific changes to specific devices. | The commentary does not identify what was actually changed between the original and the rewrite. |
