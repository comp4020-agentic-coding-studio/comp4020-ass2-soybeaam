## Instructions

> Bring one historical, legal, or cultural excuse to the class, and defend
> your reading of it under questioning.

In groups of three to four, research one case from history, law, or a culture
other than your own where an excuse shaped what happened next: a legal
defense, a diplomatic incident, a documented cultural practice of
face-saving. Present it for 10 minutes, then take questions from the room for
5.

Choose a case with documentation behind it, not just a widely repeated
anecdote. Look for a court transcript, a diplomatic cable, an ethnographic account,
contemporary press coverage. A case you can only source to a single
secondhand retelling will not survive the depth-and-accuracy criterion, and
it will show under questioning when the room asks for a detail the source
doesn't actually contain. Cross-cultural cases carry a specific
responsibility: represent the practice on its own terms, situated in its
own context, rather than flattening it into a translation of a Western
excuse-shape it may not map onto cleanly.

Structure the ten minutes around an argument, not a chronology. A
recitation of "first this happened, then this happened" leaves the room
with facts but no analysis; a presentation that opens with a claim about
which excuse mechanisms are doing the work in the case, then walks the
evidence in support of that claim, gives the audience something to test
during questions. Assign the five minutes of cross-examination real
weight in your preparation. Every member of the group should be able to
defend the group's reading of the case, not just the sub-section they
personally researched, because the room's questions will not respect the
division of labour you used to write the presentation.

## Submission Requirements

The live presentation (slides optional, but a one-page summary handed in
beforehand is not) and a group-authored one-page brief naming your sources.
The one-page brief functions as your evidentiary record: it should list
each source consulted, specify what claim in the presentation each source
supports, and flag any point in the case where the available sources
disagree with one another. Submitting a brief that merely lists reading
material without tying it to specific claims makes it difficult for markers
to distinguish a well-evidenced case from one that sounds confident but
isn't, so cite precisely.

## Marking rubric

Each criterion is judged against the scale below, not allocated a mark
count. Your grade is the band your work sits in, not an average of parts.

| Criteria | High Distinction (5) | Distinction (4) | Credit (3) | Pass (2) | Fail (1) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Depth and accuracy of the case study** | The case is verified against primary or clearly credible sources and situated precisely within the semester's taxonomy: denial, justification, and diffusion of responsibility are each identified and distinguished with evidence from the case itself. | The case is well sourced and correctly mapped onto the taxonomy, with at least two of the three excuse mechanisms identified and supported with specific detail from the record. | The case is accurate and one taxonomy mechanism is clearly identified and linked to evidence, though the mapping is thinner or partly asserted rather than shown. | The case is broadly accurate but the link to the taxonomy is asserted rather than demonstrated, or only loosely connected to the excuse mechanisms covered in weeks 6–8. | The case is inaccurate, unverifiable, or not meaningfully connected to the course's excuse taxonomy. |
| **Quality of the live presentation** | The 10 minutes are used with clear structure and pacing; every group member speaks to a substantive part of the argument, and the group anticipates and pre-empts the room's likely objections. | The presentation is clearly structured and confidently delivered, with each member contributing a meaningful segment, though pacing or transitions are occasionally uneven. | The presentation covers the case adequately within time, but structure or delivery is uneven, or one member's contribution is noticeably thinner than the others'. | The presentation is delivered within roughly the allotted time but is disorganised, reads from notes throughout, or leaves the argument's structure unclear. | The presentation is significantly under or over time, incoherent, or one or more group members do not meaningfully contribute. |
| **Handling of cross-examination questions** | The group answers unscripted questions directly, draws on the taxonomy to defend or revise their reading in real time, and distinguishes what the case study shows from what it doesn't. | The group answers most questions directly and correctly, using course concepts, with only minor hedging or repetition under pressure. | The group answers questions adequately but leans on prepared talking points rather than reasoning live, or misapplies a course concept under pressure. | The group attempts to answer questions but responses are vague, evasive, or largely restate the presentation rather than engaging the question asked. | The group cannot answer basic questions about their own case, or answers are fabricated rather than drawn from the research. |
